Thanks to NASA, we uncover how elusive "ghost" particles could hugely boost gold production

Study shows neutrino flavor changes in neutron star mergers may reshape heavy element formation and detectable cosmic signals.

Sayan Sen Neowin @ssc_combater007 · Jul 25, 2026 20:30 EDT with 0 comments

A new study by researchers from Penn State and the University of Tennessee Knoxville suggests that changes in neutrinos during neutron star mergers could play an important role in how these powerful cosmic events unfold, the signals they produce and the formation of heavy elements in the universe.

The findings, published in the journal Physical Review Letters, describe the first computer simulations to include changes in neutrino "flavors" during the merger of two neutron stars. The researchers said the results could improve understanding of how heavy metals and rare earth elements are formed, provide new insights into matter under extreme conditions and help scientists interpret future observations of these events.

Neutron stars are the extremely dense cores left behind after massive stars explode as supernovae. Although they are only about the size of a city, they can contain more mass than the Sun. When two neutron stars collide, they create some of the most energetic events in the universe. These collisions produce gravitational waves, which are ripples in space-time, and electromagnetic radiation such as X-rays and gamma rays that can be detected from Earth. Scientists also believe these mergers are one of the universe's main sources of heavy elements such as gold and platinum.

One of the key particles involved in these events is the neutrino, an electrically neutral particle that interacts only weakly with matter. Because neutrinos rarely interact with their surroundings, they can travel vast distances while carrying information about the environments where they were produced. Neutrinos exist in three forms, or "flavors", called electron, muon and tau. Under certain conditions, they can change from one flavor to another. Since each flavor interacts differently with matter, these transformations can change the balance between neutrons and protons during a neutron star merger, which in turn affects how new elements are formed.

Previous simulations of neutron star mergers did not include this process. "Previous simulations of binary neutron star mergers have not included the transformation of neutrino flavor," said Yi Qiu, graduate student in physics at the Penn State Eberly College of Science and the study's first author.

"This is partly because this process happens on a nanosecond timescale and is very difficult to capture and partly because, until recently, we didn't know enough about the theoretical physics underlying these transformations, which falls outside of the standard model of physics. In our new simulations, we found that the extent and location of neutrinos mixing and transforming impacts the matter that is ejected from the merger, the structure and composition of what remains after the merger — the remnant — as well as the material around it."

To study these effects, the researchers built the first numerical relativity simulation to include neutrino flavor transformations during neutron star mergers. Numerical relativity uses computer models based on Einstein's theory of gravity to simulate events involving extremely strong gravitational fields. The simulation combined gravity, general relativity, hydrodynamics, which describes how dense stellar matter moves, and neutrino mixing. The researchers focused on the conversion of electron neutrinos into muon neutrinos, which they identified as the most relevant transformation in these environments. They also tested different scenarios by changing when and where neutrino mixing occurred, as well as the density of the surrounding matter.

The simulations showed that neutrino flavor transformations changed the composition and structure of the merger remnant, the object left behind after the collision. According to the researchers, these changes could also leave a detectable signature in the gravitational waves produced after the merger, helping scientists better understand these events through future observations.

The study also found that neutrino transformations significantly affected nucleosynthesis, the process through which new atomic elements are formed, and the composition of the matter ejected during the merger. As neutron-rich material is thrown into space, atoms rapidly capture neutrons and eventually form heavier elements, including gold, platinum and rare earth elements used in products such as smartphones, electric vehicle batteries and other electronic devices.

"A neutrino's flavor changes how it interacts with other matter," said David Radice, Knerr Early Career Professor of Physics and associate professor of astronomy and astrophysics at the Penn State Eberly College of Science. "Electron type neutrinos can take a neutron, one of the three basic parts of an atom, and transform it into the other two, a proton and electron. But muon type neutrinos cannot do this. So, the conversion of neutrino flavors can alter how many neutrons are available in the system, which directly impacts the creation of heavy metals and rare earth elements. There are still many lingering questions about the cosmic origin of these important elements, and we found that accounting for neutrino mixing could increase element production by as much as a factor of 10."

The researchers also found that neutrino mixing influenced the amount and composition of material ejected during the merger. This could affect the electromagnetic signals, including X-rays and gamma rays, and possibly the gravitational waves produced by these events.

"In our simulations, neutrino mixing impacted the electromagnetic emissions from neutron star mergers and possibly the gravitational waves as well," Radice said. "With cutting-edge detectors like LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA and their next generation counterparts, such as the proposed Cosmic Explorer observatory that could start operations in the 2030s, astronomers are poised to detect gravitational waves more often than we have before. Better understanding how these emissions are created from neutron star mergers will help us interpret future observations."

The LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA (LVK) mission is a global network of gravitational-wave observatories that detect ripples in space-time caused by events such as merging black holes and neutron stars. Through NASA's General Coordinates Network (GCN), LVK quickly shares alerts and sky maps with astronomers worldwide, enabling rapid follow-up observations and advancing the study of the dynamic universe.

Despite the findings, the researchers said many aspects of neutrino transformations are still not fully understood. "There's still a lot we don't know about the theoretical physics of these neutrino transformations," Qiu said. "As theoretical particle physics continues to advance, we can greatly improve our simulations. What remains uncertain is where and how these transformations occur in neutron star mergers. Our current understanding suggests they are very likely, and our simulations show that, if they take place, they can have major effects, making it important to include them in future models and analyses."

The team said the simulation framework developed for the study can now be used by other researchers to further investigate neutrino flavor transformations and their role in neutron star mergers. "Neutron star mergers function like cosmic laboratories, providing important insights into extreme physics that we can't replicate safely on Earth," Radice said.

TL;DR:

  • Researchers have developed the first computer simulations of neutron star mergers that include neutrino flavor transformations, revealing that these tiny particles can significantly influence how these powerful cosmic collisions unfold.
  • The simulations show that neutrino flavor changes affect the composition of the merger remnant and the material ejected into space, potentially altering the electromagnetic signals and even the gravitational waves that can be detected from Earth.
  • By changing the number of neutrons available during the merger, neutrino flavor transformations could increase the production of heavy elements—including gold, platinum and rare earth elements—by up to a factor of 10, offering new insights into the cosmic origins of these materials.
  • The findings could help astronomers better interpret observations from current and future gravitational-wave detectors such as LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA and the proposed Cosmic Explorer.
  • Researchers say many aspects of neutrino flavor transformations remain uncertain, but the new simulation framework provides a foundation for future studies of neutron star mergers and the extreme physics they reveal.

Source: Penn State, APS
