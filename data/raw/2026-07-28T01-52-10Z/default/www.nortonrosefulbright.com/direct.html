From experimentation to adoption: The evolving regulatory framework for tokenized financial assets in the United States and Canada

Authors:

Mark Highman
 
Peter J. Wiazowski
 
Pier-Olivier Poisson
 
Stephan Ariyan
 
Sebastian Souchet
 
Jérémie Moisan-De Serres
 
Gage Raju-Salicki
 

Canada |  Publication |  July 2026

The tokenization of financial assets is no longer a theoretical exercise. It is rapidly transitioning from experimental pilot programs to early commercial adoption.

A new article from Norton Rose Fulbright lawyers examines the evolving regulatory frameworks in the United States and Canada, offering a detailed survey of the guidance, no-action relief, and interagency coordination that are defining the rules of engagement for tokenized financial assets, collateral, and custody arrangements across North American capital markets.

In the United States, a wave of agency-level action has created an increasingly detailed, if still fragmented, regulatory roadmap. Federal banking agencies have confirmed a technology-neutral approach to regulatory capital treatment, while the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has issued substantive guidance on the use of tokenized assets as collateral in derivatives markets, addressing enforceability, segregation, haircuts, and operational risk. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), for its part, has weighed in on the classification of tokenized securities, broker-dealer custody of crypto-asset securities, and the conditions under which certain user interface providers may operate without registration as broker-dealers.

Cross-agency coordination is also accelerating in the United States. Among other things, a formal memorandum of understanding between the SEC and CFTC and joint SEC/CFTC interpretive guidance regarding the regulatory classification of crypto assets collectively signal a maturing institutional effort to bring regulatory clarity to digital asset markets, including those involving tokenized financial assets.

North of the border, Canadian regulators are charting a more slow-paced but no less significant path. The article explores Project Samara, a Bank of Canada-led initiative that tested tokenized bond issuance and atomic settlement using a wholesale digital Canadian dollar, as well as the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization's new digital asset custody framework and the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions' prudential guidelines for crypto-asset exposures. While the Canadian Securities Administrators' intervention remains consultative, recent workshops and limited exemptive relief suggest regulators are actively exploring a future framework.

A comparative analysis reveals that both jurisdictions share a foundational principle—the "digital wrapper" does not necessarily alter the legal rights or regulatory treatment of the underlying asset. However, the jurisdictions differ materially in the maturity and specificity of their guidance. The article identifies notable gaps in both regimes, including unresolved questions around interoperability, settlement finality, and the use of tokenized financial assets as collateral in Canada, and highlights the competitive implications of asymmetric regulatory development across jurisdictions.
