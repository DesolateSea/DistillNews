Defense to Argue Mother Not Guilty of Infanticide due to Postpartum Psychosis

The trial for Lindsay Clancy commenced on Monday in Plymouth, Massachusetts. Clancy, 35, is expected to plead not guilty to killing her three children by reason of postpartum insanity. She faces three counts of first-degree murder and life in prison without parole, and over 200 witnesses are expected to testify over the course of six to eight weeks.

The Defense: Attorney Kevin Reddington didn't plan to deny Clancy's actions but rather defend her judicial innocence by reason of postpartum psychosis. Reddington called the case "probably the most emotionally disturbing and challenging" of his lengthy career but argued that Clancy, a former labor and delivery nurse, was undertreated and overmedicated – both factors that contributed to the severity of her mental illness and subsequent actions. Clancy's now-ex-husband Patrick is expected to testify for the defense. He claimed, "The real Lindsay was generously loving and caring towards everyone – me, our kids, family, friends, and her patients. The very fibers of her soul are loving."

The Prosecution: The prosecution argued Clancy acted with "deliberate, premeditated and extreme atrocity and cruelty," using Apple Maps to track her then-husband and "[give] herself the time and privacy needed to commit these murders."

For Context: Clancy allegedly heard "a compelling and unrecognizable singular male voice" urging her to take her own life and suggesting "her children would suffer if she was gone" on Jan. 24, 2023, in Duxbury, Massachusetts. After asking her husband to pick up dinner, she strangled her three children with an exercise band, killing Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and Callen, 8 – who passed away three days later. Clancy then committed acts of self-harm and allegedly attempted suicide, rendering herself to a wheelchair.

Patrick Clancy filed a lawsuit against his former wife's psychiatrist earlier this year, alleging Ms. Clancy's medical team "[failed to] attend to her worsening psychiatric condition." Ms. Clancy sought treatment for postpartum anxiety in 2022. The psychiatrist reportedly prescribed her over a dozen medications throughout the year – including Prozac, Zoloft, Klonopin, and Valium – to no avail, as Clancy's symptoms progressed to panic, numbness, paranoia, and suicidal ideations by December 2022. A postpartum depression program recommended she seek alternative care on Jan. 1, 2023, and Clancy then admitted herself for five days at McLean Hospital for further psychiatric care. The murders took place about three weeks later.

How The Media Covered It: This story did not amount to any distinct political biases, and outlets instead emphasized different aspects of the case. The New York Times (Lean Left bias) and NewsNation (Center) drew parallels between Clancy's case and that of Andrea Yates, who was found not guilty by reason of insanity for drowning her five children in 2001.

New York Times reported, "About one in seven women are affected by [postpartum depression], but research has been slow to investigate the causes. Postpartum psychosis is a rarer, more severe illness carrying a high risk of suicide and infanticide. Few women describe their experiences with either condition publicly, fearing condemnation in a culture in which any deviation from maternal bliss has been taboo." It highlighted recent legislation in multiple countries and the state of Illinois to lessen sentences for mothers who kill their infants due to postpartum illness.

Fox News (Right) gave voice to Attorney Donna Rotunno, who suggested the insanity plea could work in Clancy's case due to her illness and treatment history. The outlet analyzed, "Clancy's prior work as a nurse could cut both ways at trial. Prosecutors may argue that her medical background made her more capable of understanding her treatment and actions, but Rotunno said the defense could use the same fact to demonstrate the severity of her alleged psychosis."

Fox also noted that the trial's judge, William Sullivan, granted Mr. Clancy's request to shield evidence from the media, despite a "heated courtroom exchange," due to the emotional repercussions of publicization.
